Teacher notes

Written for 8th grade. Each of the 32 stops follows the same pattern: what happened, each side’s leaders and goals, one strategy or tactic explained, why it mattered, people to know, and two or three discussion questions.

Ways to use it

  • Walk the war in order. Use the timeline or the arrow keys (← →) to move from Lexington to Yorktown. Ask students to track how American fighting changes, especially before and after Valley Forge.
  • Compare tactics. Pair stops that use the same idea: flanking marches (Long Island, Brandywine), surprise attacks (Ticonderoga, Trenton, Stony Point), chokepoints (Great Bridge, Moores Creek), and envelopment (Bennington, Kings Mountain, Cowpens).
  • Legend or history? The “How do we know?” boxes and the people cards flag stories that rest on thin or late evidence (Molly Pitcher, Sybil Ludington, Pompey Lamb, “I have not yet begun to fight!”). They work well for sourcing practice: Who recorded it? When? Why?
  • Perspectives. Use the Spotlight filters to follow one group across the war. For Black Americans and Native nations, the war offered different and often opposite paths to freedom or survival, on both sides.
  • Write from a person’s view. Students pick one person card and write a journal entry or letter set on the date of that battle.

How sensitive topics are handled

  • Slavery, the destruction of Haudenosaunee towns, and battlefield violence are described plainly and without graphic detail.
  • Loyalists, Native nations allied with Britain, and Black Loyalists are presented with their own reasons for choosing a side.
  • LGBTQ+ history: von Steuben and John Laurens are included with what the evidence shows and where historians disagree. The notes explain that people in the 1700s didn’t use today’s words for identity, and that relationships between men were illegal at the time.

How this was checked

  • In October 2026, every stop was checked claim by claim against reliable sources: the National Park Service, the American Battlefield Trust, state encyclopedias, museums, the National Archives, and scholarly journals. Errors were corrected. Each battle page ends with a Check the sources list so students can verify it themselves.
  • For a few stops, the most detailed source was Wikipedia. Those are labeled “start here, then follow its sources,” which is a good habit to teach.
  • Two primary sources are linked directly: the Seneca chiefs’ 1790 letter to Washington (Newtown) and Sarah Osborn’s 1837 pension testimony (Yorktown).

Accuracy notes

  • Troop counts and losses are rounded. Sources often disagree, sometimes by a lot.
  • The map uses modern state lines to help students find places. In 1775 the lines were different: Maine was part of Massachusetts, Vermont was claimed by both New York and New Hampshire, and Kentucky and West Virginia were part of Virginia.
  • Some famous quotations were written down years after the fact. The text says so where it matters.

About the pictures

  • Every picture is public domain or openly licensed, from Wikimedia Commons. Each caption says what kind of picture it is:
Made at the timeFrom lifeMade laterImagined likenessPhoto
  • Many famous Revolution paintings, like Washington Crossing the Delaware (1851), were made decades later. They show how later Americans imagined the war, which makes them good sources for a lesson on how memory works.
  • London print shops in 1776 sold “portraits” of American generals their artists had never seen. Those are labeled Imagined likeness.
  • Most of the women, Black Americans and Native people on this map were never painted. Their cards say “No portrait from the time is known.” That gap is part of the history.
